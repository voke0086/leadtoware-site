Pre-Shipment Inspection: How It Works and Why We Welcome It

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

For a first container of sanitaryware, the best money a buyer spends is often not on freight or another fifty cents off the unit price — it is on an independent inspection of the packed goods, before the balance is paid. Most sourcing guides treat pre-shipment inspection as a formality. It is not: it is the only moment when you, or someone working only for you, can still say no while the goods are in the factory and the money is in your account. Here is what an inspection covers, what it costs, and when to book it.

What is pre-shipment inspection, and when should it happen?

PSI is a third-party check of finished, packed goods against the approved sample and the specification. There are three stages, and a first order usually needs the last two:

- During production (DUPRO) — an early check while the line runs, so a fault is fixed before the lot is made. - Pre-shipment (PSI) — after the order is produced and packed, before loading. - Loading supervision (CLS) — at the loading, confirming what goes in and how it is sealed.

The rule that matters: inspection must happen before the balance payment, not after the vessel sails. A report issued at sea is documentation, not protection — there is nothing left to reject.

What does an inspector actually check on ceramic sanitaryware?

Ceramic cannot be reworked like metal or plastic: a glaze defect or a warped casting is scrap, not a repair. So a proper checklist goes beyond counting cartons.

A report that only says "quality: good" is worthless — ask for defect photos and an accept/reject decision per area.

What AQL should you ask for?

Sampling follows ISO 2859-1, the same tables as ANSI/ASQ Z1.4. Specify three things in the inspection order, not after the report: inspection level (general level II is the trade default), AQL for major defects (2.5 is common for consumer goods) and AQL for minor defects (4.0 is common). Glaze flaws on visible surfaces class as major, minor elsewhere — say so, or the inspector decides for you.

Read the report properly: it must state lot size, sample size, code letter, and accept and reject numbers. A percentage with no sample size is a summary, not an inspection.

Who appoints the inspector, and who pays?

You appoint, you pay — an inspector chosen and paid by the supplier audits his own work. SGS, Bureau Veritas, TÜV, Intertek and independent local agents all do this; local agents are cheaper and often know ceramic defects better. Fees are quoted per man-day, not as a percentage of order value, and a container is typically a one-man-day job.

What must be ready before the inspector arrives?

The usual reason an inspection yields nothing is that the goods were not ready: the full lot produced, packed and segregated from other customers' orders; the approved golden sample on site, signed and dated; carton artwork, specification sheet and proforma invoice at hand; water and power for flush tests; and one person who knows the order present to open cartons.

Why do we welcome inspection before the balance?

Our terms are 30% deposit with the order and the balance paid before the goods leave the factory — and those terms only work when inspection and loading supervision happen before that balance falls due. The sequence is fixed: deposit, production, your inspector passes the lot, the container is loaded and sealed under supervision, balance paid, goods leave. We do not offer balance against a copy of the bill of lading: against-BL terms push payment past the ship's departure, so the inspection came too late to matter or never happened.

A factory that fears inspection has a reason: a defect caught on our floor costs a few pieces; the same defect caught at your warehouse costs a container, a claim and your season.

What does loading supervision add?

Loading is where a clean inspection gets undone. Supervision confirms the container is dry and sound, records the seal number, counts cartons model by model, and photographs the stow, including the last row before the doors close. You get the record the same day, so what you approved is provably what shipped.

What about samples and tooling?

Samples of existing catalogue models are paid, and the fee is refunded once the order is confirmed — the piece you approved becomes the golden sample every inspection is measured against. Tooling for a private OEM design is customer-funded, in the range of RMB 10,000 up to several tens of thousands depending on the item, with a minimum order quantity attached, and credited back against cumulative shipped volume. Molds always remain factory property, and tooled models are by default available to other customers; exclusivity can be agreed for long-term partners. Put it in writing before the deposit moves.

The short version

Book the inspection for after packing and before the balance. Specify inspection level and AQL in the order, and read the sample size, not the percentage. Pay 30% to start; the balance only after your inspector has passed the goods and watched them loaded.
