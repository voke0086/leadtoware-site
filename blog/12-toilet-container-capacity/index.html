How Many Toilets Fit in a Container? FCL Planning for Bathroom Importers

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

It is one of the first questions every bathroom importer asks, usually before the first quote is even requested: how many toilets actually fit in a container? The number decides your freight cost per unit, your first-order budget and your shelf plan. The honest answer is "it depends on the model" — but the range is narrow enough to plan with, and the math behind it is worth learning, because it is the same math every factory runs when it tells you how many containers your project needs.

How many toilets fit in a 20ft and a 40ft container?

Start with the boxes, not the toilets. A standard 20ft container offers roughly 33 cubic metres of internal volume (about 5.9 × 2.35 × 2.39 m). A 40ft standard container gives about 67 cbm, and a 40ft high-cube about 76 cbm, thanks to its taller internal height of roughly 2.70 m.

A two-piece toilet travels as two cartons — bowl and tank — totalling roughly 0.22 to 0.26 cbm per set, depending on the model and carton design. A one-piece toilet ships as a single bulky carton of roughly 0.25 to 0.30 cbm, and it needs more void protection inside the container. Put those facts together and you get the planning table below. Treat the figures as indicative ranges for budgeting; the exact count comes from the loading plan the factory builds for the specific models you pick.

The pattern is simple: the more compact and separable the cartons, the better the container utilization. That is one reason two-piece models remain the volume workhorse of the export trade.

What actually limits the load — volume or weight?

For toilets, volume almost always runs out first. A packed two-piece set typically weighs somewhere in the range of 40–55 kg including carton and cushioning, so even a full high-cube of two-piece sets sits comfortably under the roughly 28-tonne payload limit of the container. Weight becomes the binding constraint only when the load mixes dense goods — pallets of fittings or heavy vanity units — with ceramics, which is exactly when a competent load plan starts swapping positions instead of just stacking.

There is one more constraint buyers forget: stacking. Ceramics stack, but not infinitely. Carton design sets the number of layers a carton can safely carry, and a proper loading plan respects that limit, because crushed cartons at the bottom of a stack are how breakage claims are born.

Can one container mix different models — or different products?

Yes, and it is the standard way first orders are built. A single container can carry several toilet models across both body types, and it can mix toilets with basins, squat pans, shower trays and bathroom cabinets — the full bathroom range shipped as one load from one factory. Mixing is not a favour; it is how the minimum order of one full container is meant to be used. Buyers hedging a first order usually spread it across two or three proven two-piece models plus one one-piece hero model, then rebalance in the second round once sell-through data arrives.

Two practical notes. Mixed loads are floor-loaded piece by piece, which takes longer than palletized loading but achieves a much higher fill rate. And the mix affects the load plan more than the container count, so send your intended product mix to the factory before the booking is made, not after.

How do ceramics survive the ocean crossing?

The loading plan matters more than the sea voyage. Our standard is 5-layer export cartons with molded foam or honeycomb cushioning at every face of every component. Heavy items go in low, cartons are cross-tied and voids filled so nothing shifts when the container meets a swell, and stacking limits are respected throughout. Loading itself is photo-documented, and you or your third-party inspector are welcome to witness it. Shock indicators can be fitted on request.

Breakage claims, in our experience, are rarely about ceramic quality — the kiln either vitrifies the body or it does not. They are about what happened between the kiln and the destination port: carton quality, stacking discipline and load securing. Those three things sit inside the factory's control, which is why we supervise them ourselves.

How do I turn container counts into a freight budget?

Divide the ocean freight rate by the realistic unit count, not the theoretical one. If a 40ft high-cube carries 260 two-piece sets on paper but your chosen model is unusually bulky, the real number may be closer to 240 — and the freight per unit changes accordingly. The professional move is to ask for the exact carton dimensions and gross weights of your shortlisted models, then run the cbm math yourself, or ask the factory for a carton-level loading plan. A factory that cannot produce one has probably not loaded many containers.

The short version

Plan on roughly 110–130 two-piece sets per 20ft container and 230–300 per 40ft high-cube, adjust for the specific models, mix freely across the bathroom range, and treat the loading plan — not the sea leg — as the real quality battleground. Get carton dimensions early, budget freight on realistic counts, and make inspection and loading supervision part of the deal.
