AQL Sampling Inspection for Sanitaryware: How to Read the Report Before You Sign

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

A pre-shipment inspection report arrives as a page of numbers: lot size, sample size, Ac, Re, and a verdict. Most buyers read the verdict and stop. That is a mistake, because the same word "passed" can mean two very different things depending on which AQL you agreed and which defects the inspector was told to count. Here is how the arithmetic works, and what to fix in the inspection brief before anyone opens a carton.

What does AQL actually mean?

AQL stands for acceptance quality limit. It is not a target and it is not a guarantee; it is the worst quality level still considered acceptable under a given sampling plan. The standard behind it is ISO 2859-1, and the logic is statistical: instead of opening every carton, you inspect a calculated sample and accept or reject the whole lot on the number of defects found.

Two decisions shape everything after them. The inspection level: general level II is the default for most consumer goods and the one to name in the purchase order if you want nothing left to interpretation. The AQL values per defect class: a very common split is 0 for critical, 2.5 for major, 4.0 for minor. A supplier who proposes one AQL for everything has not thought about what actually ruins a toilet.

Which defects count as critical, major and minor?

Classification is where buyers and factories disagree most, so agree it in writing before the inspection is booked.

ClassDefinitionSanitaryware examplesTypical AQL
CriticalUnsafe, illegal or unusable; a recall riskSharp glaze edge that cuts; tank fitting leaking at the seam; wrong or non-compliant labelling for the destination market0
MajorVisible or functional fault a consumer would rejectCrack through the body; chip on the rim or seat area; wobble that cannot be shimmed; flush volume far off specification; spiral or pinhole in the glaze on a visible surface2.5
MinorCosmetic deviation within tolerance; does not affect useSlight colour variation between pieces; tiny pinhole on a hidden surface; uneven glaze texture on the base4.0

The trap is a loose definition of "visible surface". A pinhole inside the bowl below the water line and a pinhole on the front apron are both pinholes, but only one comes back to you as a return. Name the surfaces you care about: front, top, seat area, and anything within eye line of a seated user.

How many pieces does the inspector open?

Sample size follows lot size, not container count. A 40HQ holds roughly 200 one-piece toilets (a checkable figure: about 68 cubic metres of internal volume, a one-piece carton around 0.3 cubic metres), which puts the lot in code G or H, so the inspector opens 32 or 50 pieces.

Two things in that table are worth internalising. At AQL 2.5 with a 50-piece sample, three defective units still pass. And the sample is not proportional: doubling the order does not double the inspection, so a large lot can carry a higher absolute number of defects and still "pass".

What do Ac and Re mean on the report?

Ac is the acceptance number: the maximum number of defectives you may find and still accept the lot. Re is the rejection number, usually Ac plus one. Find Ac or fewer and the lot passes; find Re or more and it fails. There is no negotiating zone, which is exactly why the number written in the purchase order matters more than the argument after the report lands.

Why does "passed" still worry experienced buyers?

Because a sampling plan tests the lot, not the process. A passing report says this consignment is probably within the agreed limit; it says nothing about the next one. Two habits close that gap. First, read the defect distribution, not only the count: three chipped rims from the same mould position is a process signal, not bad luck. Second, keep a golden sample sealed and signed at the factory, and have the inspector photograph it beside the production pieces. Approved samples for catalogue models are available against a sample fee, refunded when the order is placed.

What belongs in the inspection brief?

Write it down and send it to the inspection company before booking:

1. Inspection level and AQL per defect class. 2. Dimensional and flush-volume tolerances, with the measurement method named. 3. Which surfaces are cosmetic-critical. 4. Fitting checks: fill valve shut-off, flush performance, seat stability, no seepage at the tank-to-bowl joint. 5. Packaging checks: carton strength, foam coverage, pallet pattern, export marking. 6. Photo set: carton label, pallet, loaded container with the door seal visible. 7. What happens on failure: rework window, re-inspection, and who pays for the second visit.

Item seven is where money hides. A second inspection is a real cost and a real delay, so settle in advance whether a failed lot is reworked and re-inspected, or the defective pieces are sorted out and the lot re-presented.

The short version

AQL is a sampling contract, not a quality promise. Fix the inspection level, the defect classes and the numbers in writing; remember that at 2.5 with 50 pieces, three defects still pass; read the distribution, not only the verdict; and keep a sealed golden sample to compare against. A report protects you only as far as the brief behind it.
