Peak Season Planning: How to Schedule Your Sanitaryware Orders So Lead Times Don't Slip

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Every importer of ceramic sanitaryware eventually meets the same surprise: the factory that quoted 15 to 25 days in spring suddenly quotes 45, right when your replenishment window is closing. Nothing went wrong with your order. The calendar did. Kiln capacity is a fixed line, and when the seasonal wave hits it, the queue simply grows. Buyers who plan around that calendar pay the same price and still ship on time. This article maps the seasonal rhythm of the industry and turns it into a scheduling checklist.

Why do lead times stretch at certain times of the year?

A sanitaryware factory's output is capped by its kilns, not by its workforce. A tunnel kiln runs around the clock at temperatures near 1,200°C, and its cycle does not compress when more orders arrive. When order volume rises beyond the line's daily output, the only variable that changes is where your order sits in the queue — and that is what stretches your lead time. Lead-time inflation is a queueing problem, not a productivity problem, which is why it can be planned around but never negotiated away.

When are the pressure points in China's sanitaryware calendar?

Three periods reliably tighten capacity across the industry:

PeriodWhat drives itWhat a buyer should do
January–February (Lunar New Year)Workers return to hometowns; factories wind down a week or more either side of the holiday; shipments get pulled forwardPlace orders in November–December; decide early whether you need pre-holiday shipment or early-March production
March–May (replenishment wave)Northern-hemisphere distributors restock for the selling season; mold and tooling projects crowd the lineBook production slots in advance; sequence your model approvals early
August–October (pre-retail-season rush)Christmas, Black Friday and national retail events pull container demand forward; goods must land with weeks to spareLock in slots by mid-summer; treat ocean freight booking as part of the production plan

Between these waves, spring (June–July) and late autumn typically offer the shortest queues and the most attention from the production floor — a good window for first orders, golden-sample work and anything that deserves extra care.

How do you judge whether a factory has capacity headroom?

Buyers are often told to look for a supplier with roughly 30% capacity headroom beyond current orders, so your container never fights for kiln space. How do you verify that? Expect specific answers to:

- What is on the line this month? A working factory describes running orders by market and volume. Vagueness is a signal. - When does my production start? Not "how long do you need" — "when does the line begin." The two numbers differ whenever a queue exists. - What is your daily output for my models? Output per day for a specific model family is a production number; an annual total is marketing. - What is your firing cycle? This is the hard constraint. A seller who cannot discuss kilns may be quoting from a factory they do not run. - Do you overflow to other plants in peak season? A legitimate practice, but you deserve to know whose kiln your goods come out of — and to re-approve the golden sample against the producing plant.

How do you protect your production slot?

Once a factory commits to a slot, two things keep it yours:

1. The deposit starts the clock. Slots are allocated against confirmed orders, not conversation. We work on 30% deposit with the order and the balance paid before the goods leave the factory — and that balance falls due only after open inspection and container loading supervision, when you or your inspector have seen the goods and watched them packed. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading. The deposit secures your place on the line; the balance is paid only when the finished, packed goods are in front of you. 2. The golden sample is approved before your slot begins. Scheduling should never overlap with sample iteration: approve the reference sample first, freeze it, then start the slot. A slot that starts before sign-off invites rework, and rework is the hidden lead-time killer.

Also schedule the boring parts: book the vessel as soon as the production date firms up, and confirm that fittings, cartons and pallets are already in stock — the ceramics may finish on schedule, but a missing flush valve stops a container all the same.

What about first orders and OEM projects?

First orders deserve the low season. A first order carries learning: new packaging, new labels, new tolerance discussions — work that gets attention when the line is calm, not assembly-line momentum. This is also the right window for OEM and ODM projects, because moulded products carry a development stage before production; start the tooling conversation a full cycle earlier than you think necessary. Tooling is paid by the buyer (in the range of ten to several tens of thousands of RMB depending on the product), carries a minimum order quantity, and the fee is credited back against your accumulated shipments; the mould remains factory property, and moulded products are available to other customers by default unless exclusivity is agreed for long-term partners.

Samples for catalogue models carry a sample fee, refunded when you order — so the sample stage costs you little and should be used freely, in any season.

The short version

Kiln capacity is fixed, so seasonal lead-time inflation is a queue you either plan around or wait in. Ask where your order enters the queue, secure the slot with the deposit, approve the golden sample before production starts, and book freight in parallel. Place first orders and tooling projects in the quiet window, and treat the two annual waves — Lunar New Year and the August–October retail-season rush — as dates on your buying calendar, not surprises.
