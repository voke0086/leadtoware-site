How to Verify a Chinese Sanitaryware Factory Before Your First Order

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

You have a quotation that looks right, a catalogue that looks professional, and a salesperson who replies within minutes. None of that tells you who will actually make your toilets. This guide walks through the checks experienced buyers run before paying a deposit to a sanitaryware factory in China — the same checks we run into from the other side, every week.

Step 1: Confirm Who You Are Actually Talking To

Ask for the factory's business licence and compare the registered address against the real production site. A mismatch is not always fatal — many Chinese factories register an office in a city centre — but the registered scope should include manufacturing, not only trading. If the paperwork only covers trading, you are dealing with a trading company. That is not automatically wrong, but you deserve to know who casts your ceramic.

The simplest test: ask which parts of the process are in-house. A ceramic factory that owns its own production can talk about slip preparation, mould cycles, glaze lines and kiln schedules without checking with anyone.

Step 2: Verify Certificates, Not Certificate Photos

For markets that require them, ask for the certificate number, the issuing body and the expiry date — then check them on the certification body's public database. A photo of a framed certificate in a hallway proves nothing; a verifiable number does.

Also be clear about the difference between factory-level and product-level certification. ISO 9001 says something about a factory's management system, nothing about whether a specific toilet meets your market's plumbing code. Ask what is certified for the exact model you are buying.

Step 3: Ask for a Live Video Tour

A recorded video is marketing. A live call is an audit. On a live tour you can ask to see the casting floor, the glazing line, the kiln area and the packing station — and you can ask questions the guide did not prepare for: How many kiln cars per week? Where do rejected pieces go? Who runs the final inspection?

A factory with nothing to hide will walk the floor with you. One that keeps steering you back to the showroom is telling you something.

Step 4: Lock the Sample Before the Order

Photos cannot show glaze texture, flush performance or true colour. Order a sample of the exact model, keep it as the signed reference standard, and write it into the purchase contract as the benchmark for the production batch.

At our factory, existing catalogue models are available as samples for a charge that is credited back when you place the order. For a design of your own, tooling is required: mould cost, development time and minimum order quantity are quoted after we review your drawings.

Step 5: Agree on Inspection Before Shipment — and Payment That Reflects It

Pre-shipment inspection is standard practice for a first order: an independent inspector (SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, QIMA or your own agent) checks a statistical sample against the approved specification and issues a written report with photos.

Here is the practical link to payment terms. The common structure in this industry is a deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading — which means the goods are already on the water when you pay the rest, and any problem discovered after arrival is expensive to fix. We do it differently: 30% deposit, and the balance before the goods leave the factory, because every order is open to inspection and container-loading supervision before the final payment. You settle the balance only after you — or your inspector — have seen what is going into the container.

If a supplier resists third-party inspection, that tells you more than any certificate will.

Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

Registered as trading only, presented as a factoryYou do not know who makes your goods
Certificates without verifiable numbersExpired, borrowed, or invented
Refuses a live video tour of productionThe floor does not match the showroom
No sample policy in writingStandards will drift between sample and shipment
Resists pre-shipment inspectionNobody resists inspection of good goods

What a First Order Should Look Like

Enquiry with model numbers or photos → quotation to your specification → sample approved and signed → deposit → production with progress photos → pre-shipment inspection → container loading with a full photo record → balance before the goods leave the factory. One region, one supplier, one accountable team.
