What Is a Golden Sample? The Approval Step That Protects Your Whole Order

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Most first orders fail for a reason nobody argues about: the buyer approves a catalogue page, opens the container a month later, and the glaze is a shade greener than the photo. Nothing is broken, but no document says what "right" was — and that document is a golden sample.

What is a golden sample, in plain terms?

A golden sample — also called a reference or sealed sample — is one unit from a real production run, approved in writing by the buyer, sealed, signed and dated by both parties, and held by each side for the life of the order.

The key word is *sealed*. An unsealed sample drifts: handled, chipped, cleaned with the wrong cloth, swapped by accident. A sealed unit in a signed carton with a dated approval sheet is evidence. When an inspector opens your container, the question stops being "does this look nice" and becomes "does this match the sealed unit".

Why is a catalogue photo not a specification?

Because a photograph cannot carry what buyers actually argue about:

- Glaze tone. Every kiln load shifts slightly, and studio light says nothing about how a glaze reads under showroom lighting. Tone is judged against a physical reference or not at all. - Surface and edge quality. Even glaze coverage on the rim, pinholing at arm's length, a smoothly ground underside — tactile things, described badly and heard differently. - Fit and feel. Seat alignment, soft-close speed, weight of the lid, sound of the flush. A dimension sheet will not tell you a lid feels thin. - The deviations a factory calls normal. Glaze pooling at the base, a faint mould seam in the trapway. Unless someone writes down which are acceptable, the default is all of them.

What belongs in a golden sample file?

The unit is half of it; the paperwork is what makes it enforceable.

How does the process run?

1. Request. You name the models, sizes and configuration, and we confirm what can be sampled from current tooling. 2. Sample order. Samples of catalogue models carry a sample fee, refunded when you place the order. The sample date is quoted in writing with the invoice, so the wait is a commitment. 3. Your evaluation. Install it, sit on it, flush it, look at it under your own lighting, and mark up changes with photographs. Changes here are cheap; after the mold is cut and the container loaded, they are not. 4. Sealing. Both sides sign and date the approval sheet, seal the unit, keep a copy. 5. Production against the sample. The master copy sits at the end of the line, not in a drawer. 6. Verification. Before the balance falls due, goods are compared against that unit — by your inspector, your visit, or a documented video walkthrough.

How does sampling work for OEM and ODM projects?

With a new mold the sample decides whether the project continues, so the terms must be clear before anything is cut:

- Tooling is customer-funded, with a minimum order quantity agreed in writing alongside it. A new mold is real engineering cost — ten thousand to several tens of thousands of RMB depending on size and complexity — quoted as a line item, not buried in the unit price. - The fee is credited back against cumulative shipments, with the mechanism and thresholds written into the agreement. - The mold remains a factory asset, whether the fee is fully credited or the minimum volume is never reached. Buyers sometimes expect to own a tool they paid for; the convention here is the opposite. - Mold-specific products are, by default, available to other customers — a factory that refuses every other order cannot keep a mold maintained. Long-standing customers can agree exclusivity for a defined period and volume, negotiated rather than assumed. - The pre-production sample is the approval gate: nothing enters mass production until it is signed, and it stays the reference for every later run.

How does the sample tie into payment and inspection?

Our structure is 30% deposit with the order and the balance before the goods leave the factory, sequenced after open inspection and container loading supervision. You or your inspector check production goods against the sealed sample, watch the container loaded and photographed, and only then does the balance fall due. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading: paying against a document means paying before verifying anything.

What goes wrong most often?

- Approving from a photograph — the most common cause of a first-order dispute. - An unsealed sample — a shelf decoration within weeks. - No counter-sample — the factory compares against memory instead. - Silent changes after approval — a substituted hinge or glaze batch. A sealed sample makes that a breach, not a debate.

The short version

A golden sample is one sealed, signed, dated production unit both sides keep. It turns quality from an opinion into a comparison, and makes inspection, payment sequencing and any later warranty conversation possible. Ask for it on the first order, and put the acceptable deviations in writing.
