How to Check Glaze Quality on a Ceramic Toilet: 7 Defects Buyers Should Know

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Two buyers can stand in front of the same toilet and see two different products. One sees a clean white surface and signs the order. The other tilts the unit toward the light, runs a fingertip along the rim, and finds pinholes that will stain within a year. Glaze is the part of a ceramic toilet you actually live with — and the part most easily judged wrong from a photograph.

Why does glaze quality matter so much?

Glaze is not paint. It is a glass layer fused to the ceramic body at temperatures above 1200°C during firing. Done right, the surface is non-porous, stains wipe off, and the finish survives years of cleaning chemicals. Done badly, the surface holds dirt, yellows, and cannot be repaired — a fired glaze defect is permanent, short of replacing the unit.

That is why glaze is one of the first things a professional inspector grades — and why the vocabulary pays for itself before your first factory visit or video call.

Which defects should you know by name?

DefectWhat it looks likeWhy it matters
PinholesTiny open pores across the surface, visible at an angle under lightTrap dirt and stain early; the most common cause of "yellowing" complaints
BlistersSmall raised bumps, sometimes burst openGlass layer did not smooth out; rough to the touch
CrazingHairline crack network in the glazeLets water and stains into the body; disqualifies the unit
Glaze skipsMatte, unglazed patches, often near edges or the baseExposed porous body; absorbs water and discolors
Orange peelDimpled texture like citrus skinCosmetic at best; signals poor kiln control
Color variationUnits from the same order reading differentlyKills showroom displays and project installations
Black or iron spotsHard dark specks in or under the surfaceFired-in contamination; cannot be cleaned off

Crazing and glaze skips are the serious ones — they let moisture into the body. Pinholes and spots are the ones buyers argue about most, because they show up in photographs and hotel guest reviews.

How do you check on site, without instruments?

Inspectors use three tools: a light source, a fingertip, and distance.

1. Tilt to the light. Hold the unit at an angle to a single light source at roughly arm's length. A sound glaze reads as one continuous, even reflection. Pinholes and orange peel appear as broken texture in that reflection. 2. Run your fingers. The rim, the inside of the bowl, the trapway edges, the underside of the base. Anything you can feel, your customer can feel. 3. Check where glaze fails first. Edges, corners, behind the seat hinges, the base ring, inside the trapway. Careless work pools or skips in these awkward zones. 4. Compare units, not one unit. Pull two or three pieces from the same production run. If white reads as two different whites, the batch is drifting. 5. Look at the underside. A cleanly ground, evenly glazed base tells you how the factory treats parts nobody photographs.

None of this needs equipment, which is exactly why it works over a video call: ask the salesperson to tilt the unit to a lamp and slowly move it — the camera shows pinholes and skips better than the naked eye sometimes does.

What does a factory that takes glaze seriously actually do?

- Fire vitreous china properly. High-temperature firing above 1200°C gives a dense, low-absorption body — which is what keeps glaze from crazing later. - Grade glaze in-line, not just at the end of the line, so kiln drift is caught between kiln loads rather than discovered in your container. - Work against a sealed golden sample, so "acceptable tone" is a comparison, not an opinion. (We covered the golden sample process in a previous article.) - Accept third-party inspection as routine. An inspector who grades glaze against the sealed sample finds nothing new — which is the point. - Hold certifications for the target market. Our sanitaryware carries CUPC & Watermark certification for North American and Australian requirements.

What goes wrong most often?

- Ordering from renders. A render has no pinholes; a kiln always makes some. Ask what tolerance is accepted and write it into the golden sample file. - Judging tone under mixed lighting. Daylight and showroom LED read differently. Pick one lighting condition and compare everything under it. - Treating pinholes as unavoidable. Dense glazing keeps them rare; "they all have that" usually means a cheaper kiln cycle.

The short version

Glaze is a fired glass layer, so every defect is permanent — which is why buyers should grade it before the order, not after the container. Learn the seven names above, check at arm's length under tilted light, compare units from the same run, and pin the accepted standard to a sealed golden sample. A factory that welcomes that process is telling you something.
