MOQ, Lead Time and Payment Terms: What Is Standard for China Sanitaryware

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Three lines of a quotation decide whether a China sourcing plan is workable: minimum order, production time, and payment terms. Get them wrong and the cheapest unit price on Alibaba turns into a container you cannot fill, a shipment that misses your season, or a deposit paid against terms that protect nobody. Buyers search for "sanitaryware MOQ lead time payment terms" because these are the terms most often misquoted — inflated minimums, vague lead times, and payment structures that shift all the risk onto one side. Here is what is actually standard for ceramic sanitaryware from a Chinese factory, and how we structure each term so the risk is shared.

What MOQ is standard for ceramic sanitaryware?

For vitreous china — toilets, basins, pedestals — the practical unit of trade is the container, not the carton. Ceramic is heavy, fragile and bulky; freight math and breakage risk both punish small parcels. So the standard MOQ is one full container, and the question that matters is not the order size but what goes inside it.

We accept mixed container orders: toilets, wash basins, vanity units, shower enclosures and accessories loaded together in one container, so a first order can cover a full bathroom range without committing to hundreds of units of a single model. If a supplier quotes you a per-model MOQ of several hundred pieces, ask whether a mixed load is possible — most factories can, they simply do not advertise it.

How long does production actually take?

15 to 25 days from deposit receipt is the standard window for a container of in-catalogue models. The sequence is fixed: casting and drying, glazing, firing in tunnel kilns at temperatures above 1200°C, then sorting, fitting and palletizing. The two variables buyers control are carton and artwork approval — production cannot start until printing files are confirmed — and model mix, because a container of one model moves faster through the line than a container of twenty.

Two honest warnings. First, ask when the clock starts: a "20-day lead time" that begins only after packaging approval is really a 30-day lead time. Second, ask about capacity headroom in your season. A factory running at full capacity in peak season will not say no to your order — it will quietly move it back a week. This is why we quote realistic windows rather than optimistic ones: a stable delivery date beats a fast one that slips.

How should payment terms be structured?

The standard structure in this trade is 30% deposit with the order, 70% balance before the goods leave the factory — and that second half only works fairly when it is paired with two rights:

1. Open pre-shipment inspection. You or your appointed third-party inspector (SGS, TÜV, Bureau Veritas, or an independent agent — your choice) come in before the balance is due and inspect against the approved golden sample. The factory opens its doors for this; a supplier who resists third-party inspection before payment is telling you something. 2. Loading supervision. The container is loaded under watch — carton condition, quantity counts, container seal number — with photo and video records shared the same day.

So the money sequence is: deposit → production → your inspection passes → container loaded and sealed under supervision → balance paid → goods leave the factory. You never pay the final 70% on trust, and the factory never ships a verified container on credit.

Why do we refuse balance against the bill of lading?

"Balance against copy of B/L" is a common counter-offer, and we decline it — not because it is unusual, but because it inverts the logic of the inspection. If the balance is only paid when the container is already on the water, the inspection has happened after your money was committed, or not at all. And once the container has sailed, a dispute has nowhere to go: no buyer can reject cargo mid-ocean, and no factory should want payment that arrives only because the buyer ran out of options.

Pay-before-ex-factory with open inspection puts the verification before the final payment, for both sides. It is the arrangement where neither party is asked to trust the other further than the evidence allows.

What about samples and tooling?

Samples: for existing catalogue models, samples are paid, and the sample fee is refunded when the order is confirmed — the sample you approved is the golden sample production is checked against. Tooling: a private-mold OEM design is funded by the customer, typically in the range of RMB 10,000 up to several tens of thousands depending on the item, with a minimum order quantity attached. The tooling cost is credited back against cumulative shipped volume, but the molds themselves always remain factory property, and tooled models are by default available to other customers — exclusivity can be agreed for long-term partners. Settle all of it in writing before the deposit moves.

The short version

One mixed container is a workable MOQ. Fifteen to twenty-five days from deposit is a realistic lead time — confirm when the clock starts. Pay 30% to start, and the balance only after your inspector has passed the goods and watched them loaded. If a quotation promises against-BL terms, a suspiciously low MOQ and a 7-day lead time in the same paragraph, that quotation was written to be clicked, not delivered.
