How Ceramic Toilets Are Made: Inside a Chaozhou Factory

By the LEADTO team · Chaozhou, Guangdong, China

If you have ever wondered how a ceramic toilet goes from a pile of clay to the glossy fixture in your bathroom, this guide walks you through the whole process — the same process we run every day in our own factory in Chaozhou, China's largest sanitaryware production base.

Why Chaozhou?

Chaozhou, in Guangdong province, produces the majority of China's ceramic sanitaryware. Decades of concentrated kilns, glazing workshops, mold shops and hardware suppliers sit within a few kilometers of each other. For a buyer, that means two things: deep supply capacity, and the ability to source a full container of mixed bathroom products from one region instead of several.

Step 1: Slip Preparation

Ceramic toilets start as a liquid clay mixture called slip — a blend of kaolin, feldspar, quartz and water, milled to a precise particle size. The recipe determines everything downstream: how the piece casts, how it shrinks in the kiln (typically 10–13%), and how it resists crazing years later.

Step 2: Casting in Molds

Slip is poured into plaster molds that absorb water and form a solid wall of clay. A one-piece toilet needs a more complex, multi-part mold than a two-piece model, which is one reason one-piece units cost more to produce. After casting, the green piece is demolded while still soft.

Step 3: Trimming and Drying

Workers trim the pouring channel, repair seams by hand and check every edge. Hand trimming is still the quality bottleneck in this industry — a skilled worker can tell by touch whether a rim or flange is true. Pieces then dry slowly under controlled humidity; rushing this stage causes hidden cracks that only appear after firing.

Step 4: Glazing

The dried body is sprayed with glaze, inside and out. Glaze thickness and uniformity decide the final look: a good glaze is smooth, even and easy to clean. This is also where color variants are made — the same body, a different glaze line.

Step 5: Firing

Pieces fire in tunnel or shuttle kilns at around 1,200–1,280°C for many hours. Firing turns the clay body dense and vitrified, with water absorption typically below 0.5%. After cooling, every piece passes a knock test and inspection; any piece with a crack or glaze defect is rejected before it ever reaches packing.

Step 6: Testing and Packing

Before packing, toilets are checked for flatness of the mounting flange, completeness of the glaze and fit of the seat and fittings. Pieces are packed in double cartons with foam corners, loaded in a way that survives ocean freight — a step many buyers only think about after receiving a broken shipment.
