Mixed Container Loading: One Supplier, Full Bathroom Range

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Most quotations assume you buy one thing. Project buyers rarely do. A hotel refurbishment, a distributor opening a showroom, a contractor fitting out an apartment block — each needs toilets in one quantity, basins in another, cabinets and shower enclosures in a third, and none fills a container alone. The result: several suppliers, several deposits, several inspections, and a forwarder stitching the loads at the port. Mixed container loading solves it — but only if the supplier's range is real and the loading is planned before production, not improvised after it.

Why do buyers consolidate a bathroom range into one container?

Freight is charged per container, not per model. Once you commit to a 40HQ or a 20GP, the cost of the box is fixed; what varies is how much of your range travels in it. Splitting the same volume across suppliers multiplies everything except the freight: separate minimum order quantities, production schedules that slip independently, several inspection bookings, several sets of documents, and cartons re-handled in consolidation by people who did not pack them.

Consolidation also changes your leverage: a multi-category order is a larger order, scheduled earlier and quoted tighter than scattered small ones. It gives you one specification sheet, one golden sample set, one inspection report and one loading record — which matters more than the freight saving when something goes wrong.

What can actually travel in one mixed container?

The limit is not the container but the packing and the stow. Ceramic is dense and fragile; cabinets are voluminous and light; shower enclosures are long and awkward. A workable mixed load balances weight and volume:

Product groupLoads asStowage note
Toilets (one-piece, two-piece)Heavy, dense cartonsBottom tier, spread across the floor, not stacked above shoulder height
Basins, pedestalsMedium weight, irregular cartonsMiddle tier, foam-separated, never glaze against glaze
Bathroom cabinetsLight, large cartonsUpper tier or rear, strapped, corners protected
Shower enclosures, traysLong, flat cartonsOn edge along a side wall, braced; glass boxed separately
Mixers, accessories, wastesSmall cartonsConsolidated into one marked block, never loose

The practical rule is heavy low, light high, nothing loose. In a ceramic-heavy load, volume rarely runs out before weight does; in a cabinet-heavy load the reverse is true, which is why the loading plan is calculated per order, not copied from the last one.

Is one supplier enough to fill a mixed container?

It depends on whether the supplier truly covers the range or merely resells it. A factory that only makes ceramics will fill your container with toilets and buy the cabinets elsewhere — putting you back in the same tangle of suppliers, one of them invisible. Ask, category by category, who makes the item and where.

For our part: the ceramic ware is produced in our own factory in Fengtang, Chaozhou, and our export lines are CUPC & Watermark certified; the wider range is assembled under the same order, so one specification, one inspection and one loading record cover the whole container. Where a category is not ours to make, we say so and quote it as such rather than let you discover it at the port.

How is a mixed load planned before production?

Planning starts with the floor plan, not the price list. Send the model list with quantities and destination, and the load is built in three steps. First, convert every line into carton dimensions and weight, because mixed loading is a carton-level puzzle, not a product-level one. Second, run volume and weight against the container type: does it fit a 20GP, need a 40HQ, or ship with the last rows deliberately left as air? Third, fix the stow sequence so the models needed first at destination are loaded last, nearest the doors.

That plan should reach you as a loading list before the deposit: model, carton count, carton size, net and gross weight, and the intended position in the container. If a supplier cannot produce that document, the "mixed container" is being improvised. Samples of stock models are charged and credited back against the order, so you can approve a physical sample set first.

What changes at loading when models are mixed?

More counting, and more photographs: a single-model container is checked by counting cartons of one size. A mixed container has to be checked line by line against the loading list, which is why we combine open inspection with loading supervision: every model is counted, the seal number is recorded, the stow is photographed tier by tier, and the final row is photographed before the doors close. You receive that record the same day, so the container you approved is provably the container that sailed.

Two disciplines protect a mixed load: cartons of the same model stay together, because a mixed pallet is a picking error waiting to happen in your warehouse; and anything long or glass is braced against a wall, never left floating mid-stow where the first hard braking shifts it into the ceramics.

How does payment work when the order is mixed?

The terms do not change because the container is mixed: 30% deposit with the order, and the balance paid before the goods leave the factory, after open inspection and container loading supervision. The sequence is deliberate: your inspector sees the whole range together rather than one supplier's slice of it, and the loading is witnessed before the final payment falls due. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading: once a mixed container is at sea, the argument has nowhere to land but a claim.

The short version

A mixed container works when one supplier genuinely covers the range, when the loading list is calculated before production, and when inspection and loading supervision cover the whole stow. Heavy low, light high, nothing loose, everything counted and photographed.
