Factory or Trading Company? How to Tell Who Actually Makes Your Toilets

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Quotations from China look identical: same model photos, same specs, same promise of "our factory". The difference sits behind the quotation — whether the people answering your email own the kiln, or buy from the plant next door and add a margin. Both can deliver. Only one can tell you why a batch came out with a glaze defect, and fix it before the next container.

What is the real difference between a factory and a trading company?

A factory owns production assets: molds, casting lines, glazing booths, kilns, a QC station, a loading bay. A trading company owns relationships and paperwork. The trading model is legitimate, and often efficient for small mixed orders. The problem is only when a trader presents itself as a manufacturer — then you are pricing and auditing the wrong entity.

In sanitaryware the distinction matters more than in most categories. Ceramic is heavy, fragile and slow to rework. When something goes wrong, the party who controls the kiln schedule is the only one who can recover the order.

Which documents reveal who you are dealing with?

Ask for the business licence and read the business scope line. Manufacturing activity should appear there. A scope limited to trading, import/export or "sales of sanitary ware" tells you the entity does not produce. Registration address is a weaker signal — plenty of real factories keep a sales office in the city centre — so compare scope and production address, not postcodes.

Then ask two more questions. Who is named as producer on the export documents? And can the company show an audit or social compliance report issued at the production site, not at an office? A trader can obtain such a report through its supplier, but it will name a different company — which is exactly the information you want.

What questions can only a real factory answer?

The fastest test costs one email. Ask about process, not products.

- What is your slip's firing shrinkage, and how do you compensate mold size for it? - How many casting cycles does a plaster mold run before it is retired? - What kiln type do you run, and what is the firing curve for a one-piece toilet? - Where do rejected pieces go, and who signs off final inspection? - Which glaze line runs your colour, and how do you match colour across batches?

A production team answers in specifics, because they live with the consequences. A trader forwards the question, and the reply arrives late, generic, or with numbers missing. Ask the same question twice, weeks apart, and check whether the answers agree.

Can a trading company still be the right choice?

Yes, in specific cases. If you need twenty categories from twenty industries in one container, a consolidator earns its margin. If your order is small and you want one invoice, a trader removes friction. The mistake is paying factory-level prices for trader-level control, or assuming a trader's certificate covers the plant that casts your ceramic.

A good trader tells you who produces, and lets you meet them. A bad one changes the subject.

How do you verify this without flying to China?

Three steps, in order of cost. First, an unscripted live video call across the casting floor, glaze line, kiln area and packing station — ask them to point the camera at the kiln car count board. Second, order a sample of the exact model and keep it as the signed reference standard. Third, book an independent pre-shipment inspection before the balance is paid.

If the entity is a trader, the call happens at someone else's plant, and the staff there will not know your order. That is usually obvious within ten minutes.

Where do payment terms fit in?

Payment structure is the clearest signal of who carries the risk. The common structure in this industry is a deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading — the goods are already at sea when you settle, and any dispute is settled from a distance.

We work the other way: thirty percent deposit, balance paid before the goods leave the factory. That only works because every order is open to inspection and to container-loading supervision — you, or your inspector, watch the goods go into the container, and you pay after that. No bill-of-lading payment, no paying for cargo you have never seen.

What about samples, tooling and molds if you go OEM?

For existing catalogue models, samples are charged, and the sample cost is credited back when you place the order.

Your own design requires tooling, paid by the customer — typically ten thousand to several tens of thousands of RMB depending on complexity, with a minimum order quantity attached. The tooling fee is credited back against cumulative shipped volume, so a steady programme eventually recovers it.

Two points to settle before signing. Molds always belong to the factory, including after the tooling credit is fully returned and including if the minimum quantity is never reached. And tooled products are by default available for the factory to supply to other customers; long-term partners can agree exclusivity contractually — that is a commercial conversation, not a default.

The short version

Read the business scope. Ask process questions and check the answers stay consistent. Request a live, unscripted tour. Lock a sample. Inspect before you pay the balance. Whoever survives those five steps is the party that actually makes your toilets.
