OEM vs ODM: Mold Ownership, Tooling Fees and Brand Building

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Private label is where sanitaryware sourcing gets serious. Catalogue buying protects you with someone else's decisions; OEM puts your brand on the product — and puts a mold, a tooling invoice and ownership questions between you and the factory. Most disputes we see come not from quality but from buyers who paid for a mold and later learned whose mold it legally is. Get these five questions straight before you sign.

What is the difference between OEM and ODM in sanitaryware?

OEM means production to your specification: the factory builds or adapts a mold to make the model you defined — your dimensions, your trap style, your branding on the ceramic and the carton. ODM means the factory's existing design, restyled or rebranded within limits: you choose from the moulded range, change colors, decals or packaging, and skip tooling entirely.

The practical difference is control and cost:

AspectOEM (new mold)ODM (existing range)
Design controlFull: dimensions, shape, fittings per your specLimited to the factory's moulded models
Tooling costPaid by the buyer, credited back against shipmentsNone
Time to first sampleWeeks longer: mold build, trial firings, adjustmentsStock models sampled in days
MOQHigher — the mold must earn its keepStandard container-level MOQ
ExclusivityNegotiable for long-term partnersNot exclusive; the model stays in the catalogue
Best forEstablished brands, market-specific designsFirst orders, market testing, tight timelines

Many buyers test the market on an ODM stock model, then commit to OEM once volumes justify a dedicated mold. We support both paths.

Who owns the mold in China sanitaryware OEM?

The mold belongs to the factory — always. Not after the tooling fee is repaid, not after the minimum order quantity is met, not if the project is cancelled halfway. The buyer pays for the mold's use of production capacity; the physical mold stays on the factory floor, in the factory's asset register.

Why is it structured this way? A ceramic mold is not a standalone product you could ship away — it is a working tool paired with the factory's casting lines, drying chambers and kilns, maintained by the factory's own toolroom across its service life. Ownership follows the machine that operates the mold.

What the buyer owns instead is the design and the brand: your drawings, your logo, your packaging artwork. The line: the *mold* stays ours; the *model made under your brand* is yours alone. Put both sentences in the OEM agreement.

What does tooling actually cost, and who pays?

The buyer pays the tooling fee. For a ceramic toilet, expect a five-figure RMB investment — roughly RMB 10,000 into the tens of thousands, depending on model complexity: a wall-hung pan with an integrated rim flushes through a far more intricate mold than a close-coupled two-piece. Basins and pedestals tool cheaper; cabinets are not molded.

Three conditions usually attach, and all three belong in writing before you transfer the fee:

- A minimum order quantity tied to the molded model — the mold must be amortised over real production, not sit idle after a trial run. - Credit-back against shipments: the fee is refunded progressively as your cumulative orders reach agreed volumes. Keep the schedule explicit — credit per container or per quantity milestone — and ask for running statements. - No ownership transfer: credit-back is a commercial arrangement, not a purchase of the mold. When the balance is fully credited you have recovered your cost; the mold remains factory property.

Beware any supplier promising free tooling with no MOQ: either the fee is hidden in the unit price, or the "mold" is someone else's existing tool with your decal on it.

Can you get exclusivity on an OEM model?

By default, no: a mold the factory owns outright may serve other customers — that is how tooling stays economical. What we offer instead is a staged arrangement: open molds supply anyone; for long-term partners, exclusivity on a specific model can be written into the agreement, tied to volume commitments that make it meaningful for both sides.

What protects you is brand exclusivity versus mold exclusivity. Your branding, carton design and market-specific configuration are exclusive to you by default — no factory should resell your labelled product. Mold exclusivity (nobody else buys any output of that tool) is the premium tier you negotiate once the relationship has history.

How do samples, payment and inspection work on an OEM order?

The commercial frame does not change because the model is new. Samples: stock models are charged and credited back against the order; an OEM model's first samples come off your new mold and are approved against your drawings before mass production is scheduled. Payment: 30% deposit with the order and the balance before the goods leave the factory — sequenced after open inspection and container loading supervision, so you verify the molded model in person before the final payment falls due. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading. Certification: our export lines are CUPC & Watermark certified, and a new OEM model is tested to the same standard before it ships.

What belongs in your OEM agreement?

Five clauses: tooling fee and credit-back schedule; MOQ for the molded model; mold ownership stated as factory property; default availability of the unbranded model to other customers, with any exclusivity and its volume conditions spelled out; and your brand and artwork protected as yours. A supplier who resists putting these on paper has answered your question.

The short version

OEM buys design control; the mold stays with the factory; the tooling fee is yours to pay and recovered through shipment credits; exclusivity is earned through volume. Get the five clauses in writing and OEM is the lowest-risk way to build a brand in sanitaryware.
