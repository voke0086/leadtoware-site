EXW, FOB or CIF? Choosing the Right Shipping Terms for Your Toilet Order

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Few lines on a quotation confuse buyers as much as the trade term at the bottom: EXW, FOB, or CIF. A toilet quoted FOB costs more than the same toilet quoted EXW, and CIF more again — though it is the same carton in the same container. The three terms simply move the line where the seller's responsibility ends and yours begins. Choosing the right line is not about the lowest number on the quote; it is about knowing which costs and risks you carry. Here is how the three terms work for ceramic sanitaryware, and how to decide.

What do EXW, FOB and CIF actually mean?

The three terms are defined by Incoterms, the international rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce. Each marks a handover point between seller and buyer:

TermSeller's responsibility endsTypically includesBuyer arranges
EXW (Ex Works)Factory gate, goods packed and readyProduction, export packingTrucking, export clearance, ocean freight, insurance
FOB (Free on Board)Loaded on the vessel at the named Chinese portProduction, inland trucking, export clearance, loadingOcean freight, insurance, destination charges
CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)Arrival port, goods insured and freight prepaidEverything under FOB plus ocean freight and minimum insuranceImport clearance, duties, destination handling

One caution: under FOB and CIF the seller's *responsibility* ends at that line, but you still own the goods in transit — the term allocates cost and tasks, not the risk of loss. That is what marine cargo insurance is for, and it is cheap relative to a container of ceramics.

What does FOB actually include — and what does it not?

FOB is the workhorse term of the China sanitaryware trade. A factory covers under it: inland trucking from Fengtang to the loading port, export customs declaration, terminal handling, and loading the container onto the vessel. It does not include the ocean freight, insurance, destination port charges, import duty and taxes, or delivery from your port to your warehouse.

The most common misunderstanding is destination charges. Under FOB they are always the buyer's account, and they vary enormously by country — which is why two "same" CIF prices can differ so much between Lagos and Rotterdam. A factory quoting CIF has to estimate charges it may not know well; a factory quoting FOB lets your forwarder handle what it knows best.

Which term should a first-time importer choose?

For a first container, FOB with your own freight forwarder is usually the cleanest structure:

- Price transparency. Product cost and freight cost are separated, so you can benchmark factories on product and forwarders on freight. A CIF quote bundles both, and a bundled price is hard to compare. - Control of the forwarder. Your forwarder answers to you. If the container is delayed or documents need a correction, your agent is chasing your interest. - Insurance at real value. Under CIF the seller insures at minimum cover; a buyer-side policy can be arranged at full invoice value with better claim handling for ceramics.

EXW makes sense when a China-based consolidator picks up from multiple factories — one agent, one truck, several suppliers. CIF makes sense when you have no forwarder on the origin side and want one near-landed price, accepting that the freight component carries a margin.

Your situationRecommended termWhy
First order, own forwarder existsFOBTransparent product price, your agent controls the move
China consolidator handles pickupsEXWOne truck, several factories, one coordinator
No forwarder, want one all-in priceCIFFactory books freight and insurance for you

How do shipping terms interact with inspection and payment?

Whatever the trade term, the sequence that protects the buyer is the same, and it happens at the factory before anything ships: goods are produced, an inspection window opens — your visit, your third-party inspector, or a video walkthrough — and loading is supervised and photo-documented. Only after you have seen what you are buying does the balance fall due.

Our structure works this way: 30% deposit with the order, and the balance paid before the goods leave the factory — deliberately sequenced after open inspection and container loading supervision. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading, because that removes the buyer's chance to verify the goods before paying. The trade term only decides who books the transport; it does not change this sequence. Whether you ship FOB with an inspector at the factory or CIF on our booking, insist on the same right: see the goods, then pay the balance.

The same logic applies upstream of production. Samples of catalogue models carry a sample fee, refunded when you place the order, so you inspect against the specification you approved. For OEM tooling, the tooling fee is customer-funded, subject to a minimum order quantity and credited back against cumulative shipments — the mold itself remains a factory asset.

Does the trade term change the packing standard?

It should not, and ours does not. Ceramics travel in 5-layer export cartons with molded foam or honeycomb cushioning at every face, cross-tied and void-filled whether the container is booked by you or by us. Under EXW, once the container leaves the factory gate everything is unambiguously your account — which is why we photo-document loading as standard and fit shock indicators on request.

The short version

EXW, FOB and CIF are not three prices for one product — they are three places to draw the responsibility line. Compare factories on product price, buy freight separately through a forwarder you trust, insure at full value. Whatever the term, keep payment tied to inspection: 30% deposit, verify, balance before ex-factory, never pay against a bill of lading copy.
