How Ceramic Toilets Are Packed to Survive Ocean Freight

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Ask a buyer what ruins a sanitaryware order and the answer is rarely the price or the lead time. It is opening a container at the destination warehouse and finding shattered vitreous china. Ceramic is the least forgiving cargo in the bathroom trade: it cannot be bent back, filled in, or re-glazed in the field — a cracked bowl is scrap, and a dent in a carton is a claim. Packaging is therefore not a cost line to squeeze; it is the last layer of quality control before the doors close. Here is how export-grade packing for ceramic toilets and basins actually works, and what to check before you confirm a packing spec.

Why is ceramic sanitaryware packed differently from other goods?

A toilet weighs far more than its carton suggests and carries its mass on thin, curved walls — the rim, the trapway, the base flange. Under road vibration and ocean swell, unprotected pieces touch each other and grind; a single point load on a corner concentrates the whole stack's weight into millimetres of glaze. Freight handling adds its own risks: containers are stacked, shifted, and opened in yards where forklifts work fast. So the packing system has to do three jobs at once — cushion every surface, block the pieces from moving against each other, and spread stacking loads across the carton instead of through the ceramic.

What does a proper export carton for a one-piece or two-piece toilet look like?

Good export packing is a system of layers, not just "a box with foam":

LayerWhat it isWhat it protects against
Surface filmSoft bag or film directly on the glazed bodyScuffs and dull marks from rubbing
Molded foam setPre-shaped EPS or EPE cradles under the base, over the rim, at the trapwayPoint loads on thin walls; shock on drop
Corner protectorsRigid blocks on all vertical edgesEdge chips — the most common transit damage
Inner dividerCardboard or foam sheet separating tank and bowl in two-piece setsPieces knocking together inside one carton
Export cartonDouble-wall, five-ply corrugated board, sealed with wide tapeCompression in stacked containers
Carton marksModel code, quantity, "FRAGILE / HANDLE WITH CARE", side marks for pickingWrong model loaded; rough handling

For the fittings — seat cover, hinges, flush valve, wax ring, bolts — the rule is a sealed accessory bag fixed inside the carton, never loose. A fitting that rattles free in transit becomes a missing-part claim even though the ceramic arrived intact.

How are basins and pedestals packed differently?

Basins are packed face-to-face only with a full foam sheet between the glazed surfaces, never glaze against glaze. Pedestals and bottle traps go in their own cartons, because their shape concentrates weight on small feet — the first thing to chip. Concealed cisterns and frames ship in the supplier's own cartons with the steel frame wrapped separately so the powder coating is not scratched by its own brackets.

What should happen at container loading?

The best packaging is undone by bad stowage. At loading the container should be checked dry and sound, cartons should be stacked on their printed "this side up" orientation, heavy models low and light models high, and the last rows braced so nothing shifts when the doors open at destination. This is exactly why we combine open inspection with loading supervision: every carton is counted model by model, the seal number is recorded, and the stow is photographed — including the final row before the doors close. You receive that record the same day, so what you approved in the inspection is provably what sailed.

Who carries the risk of breakage — buyer or factory?

It depends on the trade term, but in practice the argument travels badly. Once a container lands, proving where a break happened — factory floor, port handling, or vessel — is slow and usually inconclusive, and freight insurance covers loss more easily than a marginally damaged carton nobody noticed at discharge. The practical answer is prevention: packing that survives the route, inspection before the balance, and supervised loading with a photo record. That is also why our payment terms are 30% deposit with the order and the balance paid before the goods leave the factory, with open inspection and container loading supervision before that balance falls due. We do not offer payment against a copy of the bill of lading — under against-BL terms the goods are already at sea when the money moves, and a packaging dispute then has nowhere to land but a claim.

What should a buyer check before approving a packing spec?

Ask for photos of a packed sample of your actual model, not a catalogue image: the foam set fitted on the body, the corner protectors, the sealed accessory bag, and the closed carton. Confirm the carton is double-wall corrugated, that each model has its own marked carton, and that palletization or loose loading matches your warehouse. If your market charges by volume, ask how many cartons per model — packing that over-inflates carton size quietly raises your freight cost per piece. And if you buy mixed models, ask for the loading plan in advance so the heavy and light lines are stowed deliberately, not by accident.

The short version

Ceramic survives freight through layers — film, molded foam, corner blocks, dividers, double-wall cartons — plus disciplined stowage and a photo record of the load. Approve a real packed sample, keep inspection and loading supervision before the balance, and treat packaging as part of quality, not freight cost.
