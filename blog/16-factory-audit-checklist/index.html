Factory Audit Checklist for Sanitaryware: What to Verify, Zone by Zone

By the LEADTO team · Fengtang, Chaozhou, China

Most factory audits end with the buyer knowing more about the salesperson than about the plant. A supplier can answer any question you ask; it cannot fake a shop floor that runs in sequence — clay in one end, fired vitreous china out the other, with the awkward stages visible and staffed.

Bring this checklist when you visit us in Chaozhou, or run it over a video call. It follows the order a real audit takes: paperwork, floor, numbers.

What should you verify before you book the flight?

Documents cost a supplier nothing to send, which is why they are informative. Ask for four:

- The business licence, plus the legal entity name that will appear on the contract and invoice. If the quoting company and the licence holder differ, ask who manufactures the goods. - Certificates for your target market. Our sanitaryware carries CUPC & Watermark certification for North American and Australian requirements. Ask for the scope — which models are covered — not the cover page. - A test report for the model you are buying, showing flush performance and water consumption. A range-average report says nothing about your SKU. - Recent shipping documents with consignee details removed. A plant that exports regularly has these on file within minutes.

What does the shop floor prove that paperwork cannot?

Walk the line in production order and time each stage. You are not inspecting cleanliness; you are inspecting flow. Bodies waiting in a corner for glaze mean the plant is overbooked or badly scheduled, and your lead time will absorb that difference.

Two rows matter more than the rest. The sealed golden sample turns "acceptable quality" into a comparison rather than an opinion; the water test is the only stage that catches a functional defect before your customer does.

How do you audit a factory without travelling?

Ask for a live call, not a recorded walkthrough: a recording is a performance, a live call is not, because you choose where the camera goes. Request the kiln temperature recorder, the water test bench in operation, the packing line mid-shift, and the finished-goods yard with shipping marks readable. Then ask for one unedited wide shot of mould storage — that frame tells you how many models the plant genuinely runs.

How do you read capacity honestly?

Capacity is not the number a supplier quotes; it is the gap between what the plant runs and what it has committed. Ask three questions and cross-check the answers against the yard:

1. How many kiln loads per day, and what is the setting density per load? 2. What is booked for the next eight weeks, by market? 3. Which models are shared across customers, and what happens to my slot in peak season?

A plant that answers all three without leaving the room is either well managed or rehearsed. Put the peak-season commitment in writing before you pay a deposit: a supplier who cannot name a booking window is saying your order queues behind whoever shouted loudest.

Where do commercial terms enter the audit?

Ours are structured so verification comes before money moves:

- Payment: 30% deposit with the order, balance paid before the goods leave the factory. That balance falls due after open inspection and container loading supervision — you or your inspector check the goods, watch the container loaded and photographed, and only then pay. We do not work against a copy of the bill of lading. - Samples: catalogue models carry a sample fee, refunded when you order. - Tooling: a new mould is paid for by the customer, typically from ten thousand RMB into the tens of thousands depending on size and complexity, and carries a minimum order quantity. The fee is credited back against cumulative shipped volume. - Mould ownership: the mould remains the factory's property, including after the tooling fee has been fully credited. - Exclusivity: tooling developed for one buyer may by default also serve other customers. Long-term partners can agree exclusivity in writing.

A supplier who will not put these five points in a contract will not honour audit findings either.

What do buyers get wrong most often?

- Auditing the showroom, not the line. Showrooms are curated; the line is the product. - Accepting a certificate without scope. A certificate covering another model family is decoration. - Leaving terms verbal. Every audit conclusion belongs in the contract as an inspection right, a defect standard and a payment trigger.

The short version

Audit in three passes: documents checkable against public records, a floor walk in production order with a sealed golden sample in hand, then capacity questions cross-checked against the yard. Convert what you learn into contract terms — inspection rights, the defect standard, and payment released after loading supervision, not before.
